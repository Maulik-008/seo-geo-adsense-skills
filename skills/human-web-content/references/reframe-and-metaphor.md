# Reframes and metaphors

Two patterns get their own file because they are the most common and the hardest to see. Reference for §9.3 and §9.6 of the skill.

---

# Part 1: The reframe

## What it is

A construction that dismisses, minimizes, or questions X in order to assert Y. It creates the shape of insight without doing the reasoning. The reader gets a feeling of having been corrected about something, and no new information.

The pattern fails whether or not the word "not" appears in it.

## 1.1 Obvious forms

- This isn't X. This is Y.
- It's not X, it's Y.
- Not X. Y.
- No X. Just Y.
- Forget X. Focus on Y.
- Less X, more Y.
- Not only X, but also Y.
- It's not just about X, it's about Y.
- X? No. Y.
- Stop thinking X. Start thinking Y.
- X is dead. Y is the future.
- The question isn't X. The question is Y.
- You don't need X. You need Y.
- X is overrated. Y matters.
- X gets the attention. Y matters more.
- The real issue isn't X. It's Y.
- It was never about X. It was always about Y.

## 1.2 Softer forms, same structure

These are the ones that survive most edits, because they contain no negation:

- While X may seem...
- Although X appears...
- Sure, X...
- Yes, X...
- At first glance, X...
- On the surface, X...
- Most people think X...
- The common assumption is X...
- Conventional wisdom says X...
- People focus on X...
- X gets all the attention...
- X sounds right, but...
- X looks like the problem...
- Many assume X...

If the sentence then pivots to Y, it is the same pattern in a coat.

## 1.3 Pivot words that signal a reframe

Ordinary words. They fail only when performing this move:

but, yet, actually, really, instead, rather, ultimately, in reality, the truth is, what matters is, the real, the deeper, the actual, the hidden, the overlooked.

## 1.4 Across sentence boundaries

The ban does not stop at the full stop. Two adjacent sentences do it just as often:

> "Most teams think they have a hiring problem. They have a standards problem."
> → "The team's standards are unclear."

> "The dashboard looks like a reporting tool. It's really a decision filter."
> → "The dashboard filters decisions."

> "People blame the algorithm. The input data is broken."
> → "The input data is broken."

## 1.5 As a question

> "Is this a productivity problem? No. It's an attention problem."
> → "Attention is the constraint."

Use a question only when the reader genuinely has to answer it.

## 1.6 In headings

Banned: "Not a tool. A system." / "Less noise, more signal." / "Beyond productivity" / "From chaos to clarity" / "The real problem" / "What actually matters" / "The hidden issue."

Use: "The system" / "Signal quality" / "Attention limits" / "Input problems."

## 1.7 The fix

1. Delete the rejected half.
2. Rewrite the surviving claim as a direct sentence.
3. Check whether anything specific remains. If not, the reframe was carrying reasoning that was never there. Add the support, or cut the sentence.

> "It's not about the prompt. It's about the context."
> Step 1: "It's about the context."
> Step 2: "Context controls the output."

## 1.8 When contrast is legitimate

Contrast is correct when it fixes a specific factual, legal, technical, numerical, or scope error:

- "The meeting is Tuesday, not Thursday."
- "This is a civil deadline, not a criminal one."
- "The file is 12 MB, not 12 GB."
- "This applies to composition-scheme filers, not to regular registrations."

The test: is the contrast correcting a real mistake a reader might hold, or is it manufacturing one to look decisive?

# Part 2: Metaphor and analogy

## 2.1 Why this matters more than it looks

Decorative metaphor reads as craft, so it survives editing passes that catch every banned adjective. It is also where vague thinking hides most comfortably: "your onboarding is a leaky bucket" sounds like analysis and contains no fact.

## 2.2 The permission test

All five must hold:

1. The subject is genuinely unfamiliar, abstract, or technical to this reader.
2. The analogy makes it easier to understand, not merely more vivid.
3. It is shorter than the literal explanation.
4. It is accurate enough that it will not mislead.
5. The sentence sounds normal read aloud.

Fail any one, write literally.

## 2.3 Budget

| Piece length | Analogies |
|---|---|
| Under 800 words | 0 |
| 800 to 1,500 words | 1 maximum, if it passes |
| Longer | 1 per 1,500 words |

Never two in one section. Never stacked. Never extended across paragraphs unless the whole piece is built on it and the reader was told.

**Exception:** explainers written for beginners. One analogy usually earns its place there. State where it breaks down.

## 2.4 Banned setups

Think of it as, imagine, picture, it's like, it's kind of like, as if, as though, the X of Y, works like, acts like, functions as, serves as, a bridge between, a lens for, a mirror of, a roadmap for, the engine of, the fuel for, the backbone of, the foundation of, the fabric of, the heartbeat of, the DNA of, the glue that holds.

## 2.5 Banned metaphor families for abstract subjects

Journey metaphors for growth. Battlefield metaphors for work. Machine metaphors for people. Architecture metaphors for ideas. Ecosystem metaphors for business. Engine and fuel metaphors for motivation. Map and compass metaphors for strategy. Signal and noise for anything not literally a signal. Toolbox, iceberg, bridge, north star, flywheel, scaffolding, plumbing, gardening, chess, sports, and puzzle metaphors.

Use them only when the subject is literal. "Navigate the website" is fine. "Navigate the regulatory process" is not.

## 2.6 Banned metaphor verbs for abstract objects

When the object is an idea, strategy, product, brand, decision, organization, or emotion, do not use:

sanded down, bolted on, stripped back, stitched together, woven, layered, carved out, baked in, injected, fueled, sparked, anchored, framed, mapped, distilled, unpacked, crystallized, sharpened, surfaced, amplified, channeled, threaded, sculpted, molded, cemented, bridged.

Use: cut, added, removed, changed, joined, caused, showed, explained, reduced, clarified, fixed, named, listed, compared, chose, rejected.

## 2.7 Audit

Search the draft for: like, as if, as though, imagine, picture, kind of like, works like, acts like, functions as, serves as, lens, bridge, roadmap, engine, fuel, foundation, fabric, glue, backbone, DNA.

Each hit: run the permission test. If it fails, delete the metaphor and write the literal claim. If the literal claim turns out to be empty, that is the actual problem.

## 2.8 Worked examples

> "The product is a bridge between teams."
> → "The product lets sales and support see the same customer notes."

> "The strategy is a compass."
> → "The strategy says which customers to ignore."

> "Their tech stack is held together with duct tape."
> → "Three services still run on the 2019 API, which the vendor stopped patching in March."

Notice the pattern: the literal version is always more useful, and often reveals that the writer needs a fact they do not yet have. That is a `[TK]`, not a metaphor.
