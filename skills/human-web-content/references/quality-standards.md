# Quality standards reference

Google's published guidance, treated as a quality floor rather than a target to game. Reference for §10 of the skill.

Source: Google Search Central, "Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content" (last updated 10 December 2025). Verify against the live page for anything decisive, since the guidance is revised periodically.

---

## Content and quality questions

Google asks creators to self-assess against these:

- Does the content provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis?
- Does it provide a substantial, complete, or comprehensive description of the topic?
- Does it provide insightful analysis or interesting information beyond the obvious?
- If it draws on other sources, does it avoid simply copying or rewriting them, and instead provide substantial additional value and originality?
- Does the main heading or page title give a descriptive, helpful summary of the content?
- Does the title avoid exaggerating or being shocking?
- Is this the sort of page you would bookmark, share, or recommend?
- Would you expect to see this content in, or referenced by, a printed magazine, encyclopedia, or book?
- Does it provide substantial value compared to other pages in search results?
- Does it have spelling or stylistic issues?
- Is it produced well, or does it look sloppy or hastily produced?
- Is it mass-produced by or outsourced to a large number of creators, or spread across a network of sites, so individual pages get little attention or care?

The fourth question is the one that decides most rewrites. Rewording a source is explicitly named as insufficient. The bar is substantial additional value.

---

## Expertise questions

- Does the content present information in a way that makes you want to trust it, through clear sourcing, evidence of expertise, and background about the author or the site?
- If someone researched the publishing site, would they find it well-trusted or recognized as an authority on its topic?
- Is the content written or reviewed by an expert or enthusiast who demonstrably knows the topic well?
- Does it have easily verified factual errors?

---

## People-first questions

- Is there an existing or intended audience who would find this useful if they came to the site directly?
- Does the content demonstrate first-hand expertise and depth of knowledge?
- Does the site have a primary purpose or focus?
- After reading, will someone feel they learned enough to achieve their goal?
- Will they feel they had a satisfying experience?

---

## Search-engine-first warning signs

Answering yes to these is a warning sign, per Google:

- Content made primarily to attract search visits.
- Producing lots of content across many different topics hoping some ranks.
- Using extensive automation to produce content across topics.
- Mainly summarizing what others say without adding much value.
- Writing about things because they are trending rather than because the audience wants them.
- Content that leaves readers needing to search again for better information.
- Writing to a particular word count because of a belief Google prefers one. Google states plainly that it does not.
- Entering a niche without real expertise, purely for traffic.
- Promising to answer a question that has no answer, such as an unconfirmed release date.
- Changing dates to look fresh without substantially changing the content.
- Adding or removing large amounts of content mainly to seem fresh. Google states this does not work.

## E-E-A-T

Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness. Google states that trust is the most important of the four, that the others contribute to trust, and that content does not necessarily need to demonstrate all of them. Some content is helpful because of experience; other content is helpful because of expertise.

YMYL topics, meaning those that can significantly affect health, financial stability, safety, or societal wellbeing, are held to a higher standard.

E-E-A-T is not itself a ranking factor. It describes a set of qualities that Google's systems try to identify through other signals.

---

## Who, How, Why

**Who.** Is authorship self-evident? Do pages carry bylines where expected? Do bylines lead to real background about the author and their subject areas?

**How.** Readers benefit from knowing how content was produced. For reviews, that means how many products were tested, how, and with what evidence. Where automation or AI substantially generated content, Google suggests disclosure where a reader might reasonably wonder how it was made, along with background on how and why automation was used.

**Why.** The most important question. Content should exist primarily to help people. Using automation to produce content primarily to manipulate search rankings is a spam policy violation.

## Ad eligibility context

If the user's goal is AdSense approval or recovering from a "low value content" rejection:

**Never promise approval.** No writing method guarantees it. Reviews involve human and automated assessment of the whole site, not individual pages, and the criteria are not fully published.

**What the rejection generally means.** The site does not yet offer enough original, useful, trustworthy content, or it reads as built primarily to display ads rather than to serve readers. The review looks at the same underlying qualities as the helpful content standard.

**Common myths worth correcting when they come up:**
- "Every post needs 1000+ words." Not a Google rule. Padding makes thin content worse, not better, because the padding itself is the signal.
- "You need 15 to 20 posts." Widely repeated, not published by Google. A small number of genuinely valuable pages on a focused topic is a stronger position than twenty padded ones.
- "Add stock images to fix low value content." Decorative images add nothing. Images that carry information do.

**Site-level factors that also matter and are outside the writing itself:**
- A clear, single site purpose that a visitor can identify within seconds.
- Real About and Contact pages with genuine information.
- Author identity and background.
- Privacy policy and, where relevant, terms and disclosure pages.
- Working navigation with no empty categories or placeholder pages.
- No half-finished sections, lorem ipsum, or default theme content.
- Reasonable load speed and mobile usability.

Mention these once when the user's goal is ad eligibility, then return to the content work. The content work is identical either way, because the quality being assessed is the same quality that makes the site worth visiting.

## Practical site-level checklist

Use when auditing a whole site rather than one page.

**Purpose**
- [ ] A visitor can state what the site is about within ten seconds.
- [ ] The topic set is coherent rather than scattered.
- [ ] There is a plausible audience who would come here directly.

**Content**
- [ ] Each page has a reason to exist beyond a keyword.
- [ ] No page is a reworded version of another site's page.
- [ ] No two pages on the site cover the same ground.
- [ ] No empty categories, stub pages, or placeholder posts.
- [ ] Time-sensitive pages are dated and current.

**Trust**
- [ ] Author identity is visible and leads somewhere real.
- [ ] Sources are cited and linked where claims need support.
- [ ] About and Contact pages contain real information.
- [ ] Required policy pages exist.
- [ ] Any affiliate or sponsorship relationship is disclosed.

**Experience**
- [ ] Navigation works and reflects the actual content structure.
- [ ] The site is usable on a phone.
- [ ] Content is not buried under interstitials or ad density.

**Honesty**
- [ ] Nothing on the site claims experience the publisher does not have.
- [ ] Titles describe the content without exaggerating.
- [ ] YMYL content, if present, is sourced, dated, and appropriately cautious.

## Spam policies: the hard line

Distinct from the quality guidance above. The self-assessment questions describe content that ranks well. The spam policies describe content that gets removed or demoted regardless of how it reads.

Three policies were added in March 2024 and still govern in 2026.

### Scaled content abuse

Google's definition: generating many pages for the primary purpose of manipulating Search rankings and not helping users. The policy text states it applies "no matter how it's created."

That clause does the work. AI-generated, human-written, scraped, spun, templated, and outsourced bulk content are treated identically. The trigger is purpose and outcome, not production method. A hundred hand-written thin pages violate it; a hundred AI-assisted pages with real editorial work behind them do not.

Recognizable patterns:

- High-volume publishing across keyword clusters with no editorial review.
- Location or entity templates where only a variable changes between pages.
- Near-duplicate pages with identical structure and no distinct information.
- Multiple sites used to disguise the scale of one content operation, which is explicitly named.
- Pages that technically answer a query but restate what already ranks.

### Expired domain abuse

Buying a lapsed domain to exploit its accumulated reputation by hosting low-value content. Using an old domain for a genuinely new, people-first site is fine.

### Site reputation abuse

Publishing third-party pages on a trusted host domain with little or no first-party oversight, to take advantage of the host's ranking signals. Often called parasite SEO. Google updated this policy in November 2024 and again in August 2026.

### What this means for a publisher

- Volume is not the violation. Volume without per-page value is.
- There is no published safe number of pages, safe word count, or acceptable AI percentage. Anyone quoting one is guessing.
- Editorial review before publishing is the practical dividing line. It is also what Google's "How" guidance asks about.
- Recovery from a scaled-content problem means pruning or consolidating the thin set, enriching what survives, and then waiting. Google has said it needs to see a clean site over an extended period.

## AI-assisted content and ad eligibility

Google does not prohibit AI-assisted content in Search or in AdSense. Its published position is that generative AI is useful for research, structure, analysis, and drafting, and that AI-assisted content can rank when it is helpful, reliable, original, and made for people.

What is prohibited is using automation to produce content primarily to manipulate rankings, which falls under the scaled content policy.

For ad review specifically, the practical framing is that reviewers protect advertisers. Advertisers do not want ads beside content that is repetitive, shallow, untrustworthy, or produced at scale without oversight. The question is never "was AI used." It is whether the site would be worth visiting without ads.

Two pieces of widely circulated advice to reject:

- **"Run it through an AI humanizer before publishing."** This changes surface statistics without adding information. It does not address originality, accuracy, or usefulness, which is what is actually being judged. It can also introduce errors into text that was correct.
- **"Disclose nothing, or disclose everything."** Neither. Google's guidance suggests disclosure where a reader would reasonably wonder how the content was made, which is a judgment call about reader expectation, not a universal rule.
