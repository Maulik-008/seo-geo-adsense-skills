# Human Web Content

Write pages a real person would be glad they read.

This skill produces original, useful, human-sounding web content, and repairs content that reads as machine-generated, thin, or generic. It is built around one test: **would this page be worth publishing if there were no search engine and no ads?** Everything else follows from that.

---

## 1. Purpose and scope

Use this skill to:

- Write new articles, guides, explainers, comparisons, reviews, FAQs, and page copy.
- Rewrite weak existing content into something substantially more valuable.
- Improve specific weak parts of otherwise-decent content without destroying what works.
- Audit a page and report what is wrong, without rewriting.

Do not use this skill to generate bulk pages on topics the site has no reason to cover. That is the exact pattern Google's systems are built to catch, and it is also just bad publishing.

### The priority order

When rules conflict, resolve in this order:

1. **Truth.** Never invent facts, stats, quotes, or experiences.
2. **Usefulness.** The reader must leave better off.
3. **Originality.** Real thinking, not a reshuffle of what already ranks.
4. **Reader experience.** Clear, scannable, well-paced, honest.
5. **Natural human writing.** Voice and rhythm that sound like a person.
6. **Structure.** Organization that fits this topic, not a template.
7. **SEO.** Applied last, to content that already deserves to rank.

SEO never overrides usefulness. Ad eligibility never overrides usefulness. "Sounding human" never overrides usefulness. A page that sounds beautifully human and teaches nothing has failed.

## 2. Core principles

**Write from a position.** Content that lays out every side and commits to nothing rarely satisfies anyone. Take a view where a view is warranted, and say why.

**Specificity is the whole game.** "The integration improved efficiency" is filler. "The integration cut deploy time from 40 minutes to 4" is content. Names, numbers, dates, versions, prices, mechanisms, edge cases, and worked examples are what separate a real page from a summary of other pages.

**Apply the portability test.** If a sentence could be moved unchanged onto a different company's site, a different country, or a different product, it is probably filler. Replace it with a fact, a mechanism, a consequence, an example, or a judgment specific to this subject.

**Show, don't announce.** Do not tell the reader that something is important, surprising, or often overlooked. Demonstrate it and let them conclude that.

**Name the difference that makes the difference.** Whenever you say A differs from B, or that one option is exempt, newer, safer, or unaffected, name the part, version, date, mechanism, or rule change that makes it so. "The 2023 models are unaffected" is an assertion. "The 2023 models shipped with the revised controller, which does not use the affected firmware path" is a fact. If you do not have that detail, do not imply the difference exists.

**Answer the actual question early.** If the reader came with a question, answer it in the first hundred words, then explain, qualify, and expand. Do not hold the answer hostage behind six hundred words of context.

**Length follows the topic.** A question with a two-paragraph answer gets two paragraphs. Never pad to hit a word count. Google has stated plainly that it has no preferred word count, and padding is one of the clearest signals of content built for machines rather than readers.

**Say what you do not know.** Honest limits build more trust than confident vagueness. "This depends on your GST registration status, and the rules differ for composition scheme filers" is more useful than a smooth answer that quietly covers only one case.

**Human writing comes from editorial judgment, not from tricks.** It is not short sentences, random contractions, sprinkled "I think," or deliberately imperfect grammar. It is a writer deciding, sentence by sentence, what the reader needs next and how much explanation it deserves. That decision-making is what produces natural variation. Variation copied without the judgment behind it reads just as mechanical.

**Never write to fool a detector.** AI detectors are unreliable and guessing at them corrupts the writing. Write well instead. Good writing passes for what it is because it is genuinely good.

## 3. Research methodology

Never start drafting a factual page from memory alone.

**Step 1: Establish what the page is for.**
Who is the reader, what did they type or think before landing here, and what do they need to be able to do afterwards? Write this down in one sentence before drafting. If you cannot, ask the user.

**Step 2: Find out what already exists.**
Search the topic. Read the pages that currently answer it well. The point is not to imitate them. The point is to find where they are thin, outdated, vague, contradictory, or silent. That gap is where your page earns its place.

**Step 3: Go to primary sources for anything that matters.**
Official documentation, the vendor's own pricing page, the government notification, the standards body, the original study, the release notes. Secondary blog summaries are frequently wrong and frequently stale. If a claim would embarrass the site if wrong, verify it at the source.

**Step 4: Note the date on everything time-sensitive.**
Prices, tax rules, plan limits, version numbers, deadlines, and eligibility criteria all rot. Record when the fact was true and say so in the page where it matters.

**Step 5: Collect concrete material before writing.**
Gather at least a handful of specifics: real numbers, real names, a real scenario, a real failure mode, a real trade-off. If research produces nothing concrete, the topic is either too generic to be worth a page or needs narrowing.

**Step 6: Decide honestly what kind of authority this page has.**
Three legitimate kinds:
- *First-hand experience*, if the user actually has it. Ask them for it. Their real details are the most valuable input available.
- *Synthesis*, where the value is in pulling scattered, technical, or contradictory sources into one clear, correct explanation. This is legitimate and often genuinely useful.
- *Reference*, where the value is accuracy, completeness, and easy lookup.

Never fake the first one. Writing "when I tested this" about something nobody tested is a fabrication, it is the specific thing that destroys trust when caught, and it is not necessary. Synthesis done well beats invented experience every time.

## 4. Writing methodology

### 4.1 Before drafting

Write a one-line answer to each:

1. What is the single question or job this page handles?
2. Who is reading, and what do they already know?
3. What can this page say that the current top results do not?
4. What structure does *this* topic want? (see §4.2)
5. What are the three to five concrete specifics that must appear?

### 4.2 Choose the structure from the topic

Do not use one template for everything. Pick whichever of these actually fits, or build another:

| Topic shape | Structure |
|---|---|
| A direct question with a real answer | Answer, then why, then exceptions |
| Something broken | Symptom, causes, diagnosis, fixes ordered by likelihood |
| A decision between options | Criteria that matter, honest comparison, who should pick what |
| A process | Prerequisites, numbered steps, what goes wrong at each |
| An unfamiliar concept | Plain definition, why it exists, how it works, where it is used |
| A product or tool | What it does, who it suits, what it costs, where it falls short |
| Scattered official rules | Reference layout, clearly sectioned, with source links |
| A lived situation | Specific observation, what it taught, what generalizes |

When writing several articles for one site, deliberately vary the structures. A site where all thirty posts open the same way and end the same way looks mass-produced, because it is.

### 4.3 Introductions

Get to the point. The first paragraph should do at least one of these:

- Answer the question outright.
- State the specific problem in the reader's own terms.
- Give the one piece of context that makes the rest make sense.
- Set an honest expectation of what the page covers and what it does not.

Never open with "In today's fast-paced world," "In the ever-evolving landscape of," "Whether you are a beginner or an expert," or a paragraph explaining that the topic is important. If the reader is here, they already believe it is important.

### 4.4 Paragraphs and rhythm

Paragraph length should track how much explanation an idea needs. A simple point gets one or two lines. A developed argument gets four or five. This produces natural variation without forcing it.

Two failure modes to avoid equally:

- Every paragraph the same size, in even visual blocks.
- Every paragraph one line, chopped for false drama.

Vary sentence length because the ideas vary, not on a schedule. A short sentence lands a conclusion. A longer one carries a chain of reasoning or a qualified condition through to its end. Read the draft aloud in your head. If the rhythm never changes, the thinking probably is not changing either.

**Watch for parataxis.** Chains of short declarative sentences with no connective tissue are their own machine signature, and they are what most "write shorter sentences" advice produces. Three or more blunt statements in a row, each a separate sentence, usually means the relationships between the ideas were never worked out. Connect them with because, although, when, if, which, or so, and let the syntax carry the reasoning. Reserve the stacked-fragment rhythm for the rare moment it earns.

### 4.5 Sentences and words

- Active voice. "The team shipped it Tuesday," not "the decision emerged."
- Strong verbs. "Decided," not "made a decision." "Can," not "has the ability to."
- Prefer "is" and "has" over inflated substitutes. Not "serves as a solution for," just "is."
- Concrete nouns over abstract ones. "Refund," not "resolution pathway."
- One idea per sentence when the idea is hard. Combine only when the connection is the point.
- Repeat the right word instead of cycling synonyms. If it is a refund, call it a refund every time.

### 4.6 Headings

Headings should describe what is under them accurately enough that the page can be navigated from the headings alone. Write them as the reader would ask the question. Do not add headings to break up text or to increase the section count. A two-sentence section usually should not have its own heading.

Never write a heading and then immediately restate the heading as the first sentence.

Four heading shapes to avoid, because they name a feeling rather than the contents:

- Narrative framing: "The Right to Repair Trap"
- Thriller mystery: "The Hidden Cost of Serialization"
- Clickbait: "Why Every Publisher Is Getting This Wrong"
- Vague abstraction: "Broader implications", "Wider context", "The bigger picture", "What this means"

A heading names the subject, not the interpretation. "Economic effect of shorter product lifespans" beats "The hidden cost of planned obsolescence." Also skip parenthetical clarifications in headings; trust the reader.

### 4.7 Examples and explanation

An example should do work the explanation cannot. Use one when:

- The concept has a threshold, edge case, or condition that abstract language blurs.
- A number makes the scale real.
- The reader is likely to apply it wrongly.

Skip decorative examples that just repeat the point in story form.

### 4.8 Conclusions

End on the last useful thing. Options that work:

- A concrete recommendation.
- The next action, with enough detail to take it.
- The main caveat the reader should carry away.
- An honest open question.

Do not summarize the article. The reader was just there. Do not end on a motivational line, a metaphor, or a mic drop. If a closing sentence could end any article on any topic, delete it.

## 5. Rewriting and improving existing content

Rewriting is not synonym substitution. A page that says the same thin things in different words is still thin.

### 5.1 Assess before touching anything

Read the whole piece. Then answer:

- What is the core point, and does the page actually deliver it?
- Which parts are genuinely useful? (These get preserved.)
- Which parts are filler, restatement, or padding? (These get cut.)
- What important thing is missing that the reader will need?
- What is stale, wrong, or unverifiable?
- What is the writer's real voice, and which traits are worth keeping?

### 5.2 Then act in this order

1. **Cut** what adds nothing. Padding, restated points, generic advice, throat-clearing sections, decorative headings.
2. **Verify** every factual claim that matters. Fix or remove what fails.
3. **Add** the missing substance. This is the step that creates real value: the specific numbers, the exception nobody mentions, the step that fails silently, the actual cost.
4. **Restructure** only if the current order genuinely hurts comprehension. If you reorganize, say why.
5. **Rewrite** at sentence level for clarity and rhythm.
6. **Preserve** the writer's voice. A rough draft with a real personality should still sound like that person afterwards. Do not sand distinctive lines into neutral professional prose. Keep bluntness, humor, admitted uncertainty, and digressions that carry character.

### 5.3 Minimum effective edit

When the user asks to *improve* rather than *rewrite*, change only what is failing. Leave strong human sentences completely alone. Report what you changed and why.

### 5.4 Originality rules

- Never copy distinctive phrasing from a source, even one sentence.
- Never assemble a page from sentences taken across several sources.
- Understand the material well enough to explain it in your own structure and words. If you cannot, research more.
- Cite and link sources for data, official rules, and quotes. Attribution is a trust signal, not a weakness.
- Adding value means adding explanation, correction, organization, examples, or judgment. It does not mean rewording.

## 6. Fact-checking rules

- Do not invent statistics, dates, prices, percentages, study findings, or quotes.
- Do not attribute claims to "experts," "studies," "reports," or "many people." Name the source or drop the claim.
- Do not state a product's current pricing, limits, or features from memory. Check.
- Separate clearly: verified fact, reasonable inference, common practice, and personal opinion. The reader should always know which one they are reading.
- If the user asks for a claim you cannot verify, say so and offer the verified version instead of inventing support.
- On health, finance, legal, safety, and other high-stakes topics, hold a stricter bar: primary sources only, explicit dates, explicit jurisdiction, and an honest note about when to consult a professional.
- Include a "last verified" or "as of" note for anything time-sensitive.

**Attributions carry legal risk, not just accuracy risk.** Never write that a named person, company, or organization said, argued, believed, or admitted something unless it traces to a real document, transcript, public statement, or report. Do not infer someone's position from their role, party, employer, or reputation and then write it as their position. An invented attribution to a real person is a fabrication and a defamation exposure for the site. Write what they actually did, with the source: not "Senator Smith has argued that X is essential" but "Senator Smith co-sponsored the X Act in January 2024."

**Quote exactly or paraphrase openly.** Every word inside quotation marks must match the source. Do not fix grammar, change a plural, swap a pronoun, or tidy the phrasing. Mark any necessary alteration in square brackets. If the original wording is awkward, paraphrase without quotation marks instead. Name the speaker and the source when introducing a quote. Set off long quotations as their own block so the source's voice stays visually separate from yours.

**Do not narrate the research process.** Report what you can support and silently omit the rest. Never write "no record could be found," "specific details are limited," "as of my last update," "this information was not available," or a list of the documents you could not obtain. Do not attach an "as of [date]" qualifier to your own inability to find something. If a fact cannot be supported, cut it. The reader does not need a report on what you looked for.

## 7. Voice

Default voice: clear, practical, calm, specific, confident without swagger. A knowledgeable person explaining something to someone who needs it, not a brand talking at a market.

Adjust deliberately by context:

- **Technical documentation:** precise, neutral, dense with specifics.
- **How-to guide:** direct, second person, step by step, warns about failure points.
- **Opinion or analysis:** first person is fine, positions are stated plainly and defended.
- **Explainer for beginners:** slower, more analogies, no jargon without definition.
- **Product page:** honest about who it is not for. This builds more trust than superlatives.

Avoid corporate marketing register unless the page genuinely is marketing, and even then keep the claims checkable.

Keep phrases like "I think," "probably," or "to be honest" when they express real uncertainty or the writer's natural rhythm. Cut them when they are filler hedges. "It could potentially possibly be argued that" is noise; "I think the free plan is enough for most people" is a real position.

## 8. Indian English guidance

Use when the audience is Indian, or when the user asks for it.

**The target is a good Indian writer, not a caricature of one.** Natural, clear, competent Indian English written by a real person.

### Do

- Use Indian context when it is genuinely relevant: rupee amounts, GST and income tax realities, UPI and NEFT, Aadhaar and PAN, Indian bank and telecom names, state-level differences, Tier 2 and Tier 3 city conditions, monsoon and power-cut realities, exam and admission cycles.
- Use vocabulary and framing familiar to Indian readers rather than defaulting to American examples. Compare against services people here actually use.
- Use Indian number formats where readers expect them (lakh, crore) alongside the numeric form when clarity needs it.
- Use straightforward, unfussy explanation. Indian technical and educational writing at its best is plain and practical, and that is a strength.
- Use Indian spelling conventions consistently, whichever the site has chosen. British-influenced spellings are standard in most Indian publishing.

### Do not

- **Do not insert grammatical errors to sound Indian.** Do not write "It is giving good results" for a habitual meaning, "discuss about," "revert back," or "kindly do the needful" as flavor. These are errors or business-letter clichés, not voice.
- Do not sprinkle "basically," "actually," "honestly," "no need to worry" as ethnic markers. Use them only where a natural speaker would, which is rarely.
- Do not use canned filler lines like "Nowadays many people face this issue" or "This feature helps a lot." They are empty in any dialect.
- Do not force Indian references into topics where they are irrelevant.

Indian English is a full, standard variety of English. Writing it well means writing correctly and naturally, with Indian frames of reference. It does not mean writing worse English.

## 9. Anti-slop rules

These are the patterns that make writing read as machine-generated. Remove them on sight. Full catalogue with examples: `references/anti-slop-patterns.md`.

### 9.1 Words that are almost always wrong

delve, leverage, utilize, facilitate, foster, empower, streamline, harness, embark, elevate, unlock, supercharge, revolutionize, robust, seamless, cutting-edge, comprehensive (as filler), transformative, pivotal, paramount, multifaceted, meticulous, intricate, tapestry, realm, beacon, game-changer, paradigm shift, ever-evolving, deep dive, actionable insights, thought leadership, move the needle.

Use one only if it is the precise, ordinary word for the thing. "Robust" is fine in statistics. "Comprehensive" is fine describing an actually comprehensive index.

### 9.2 Phrases that delay the point

it is important to note, it is worth noting, in today's world, in the age of, when it comes to, at the end of the day, at its core, the truth is, the reality is, in order to, needless to say, let's dive in, here's what you need to know, without further ado, in this article we will.

Cut and state the point.

### 9.3 Structural patterns to remove

- **The reframe.** Any construction that dismisses X in order to assert Y. "It's not X, it's Y" is the obvious form, but the family is much larger and includes versions with no "not" in them at all: "While X may seem...", "Most people think X...", "X gets all the attention...", and the same move split across two sentences or asked as a question. State Y directly and delete the rejected half. Full catalogue in `references/reframe-and-metaphor.md`; this is the most common single defect in machine-written web copy.
- **Faux insight.** "What most people get wrong," "here's what nobody tells you," "the part everyone misses." Cut the setup, keep the claim.
- **Colon reveal.** Noun phrase, colon, dramatic fragment. Write a sentence instead.
- **Trailing -ing clauses that fake depth.** "...marking a pivotal moment," "...highlighting the shift," "...showcasing the commitment." Cut, or replace with the actual consequence.
- **Importance puffery.** "Stands as a testament to," "plays a vital role in," "underscores the significance of." State the fact.
- **Metadiscourse.** "That matters more than it sounds," "as you can see," "the key point is." Delete or replace with support.
- **Weasel attribution.** "Experts agree," "studies show," "industry reports suggest." Name it or cut it.
- **Rule of three.** Forced triplets of adjectives or abstract nouns. Use the one that is true.
- **Synonym cycling.** Rotating terms to avoid repeating a word.
- **Rhetorical setups.** "What if I told you," "Think about it:", "Plot twist:", self-answered questions.
- **Fake-profound kicker.** The final metaphor or aphorism. Delete it and end on the last concrete sentence.
- **Recap ending.** "In conclusion," "Ultimately," "To summarize," or a final paragraph restating the piece.
- **Sycophantic opener.** "Great question!", "Absolutely!", "Certainly!"
- **Cutoff disclaimers.** "As of my last update," "based on available information." Verify, or say plainly that it changes and link the source.

### 9.4 Formatting slop

- Bold sprinkled mid-sentence for emphasis. Bold is for genuinely critical warnings, and rarely.
- Emoji in headings.
- Bullet lists where two sentences of prose read better. Lists are for genuinely parallel items.
- Headings over two-sentence sections.
- Tables with a column of "N/A" or "Varies."

### 9.5 Em dashes

Not banned, but never a rhythm crutch. In short copy use none. In a long article one or two are acceptable where they clearly beat a comma, a full stop, or brackets. Remove clusters. If more than two appear, the sentences need restructuring, not repunctuating.

### 9.6 Analogy and metaphor control

Decorative metaphor is one of the loudest machine signatures and one of the least noticed, because it feels like craft.

Use an analogy only when all five hold:

1. The subject is genuinely unfamiliar, abstract, or technical.
2. The analogy makes it easier to understand, not just more vivid.
3. It is shorter than the literal explanation.
4. It is accurate enough not to mislead.
5. The sentence still sounds normal read aloud.

Budget: none in anything under 800 words. At most one per 1,500 words after that, and never two in one section. Never stack or extend a metaphor across paragraphs unless the piece is explicitly built on it.

Ban outright for abstract subjects: journey, battlefield, iceberg, DNA, backbone, north star, flywheel, engine, fuel, glue, bridge, lens, roadmap, plumbing, scaffolding, ecosystem for business, signal and noise for anything that is not literally a signal.

Ban the metaphor verbs when the object is an idea, strategy, brand, decision, or organization: baked in, bolted on, stitched together, woven, layered, carved out, distilled, unpacked, crystallized, sharpened, surfaced, amplified, anchored, cemented, sculpted. Use the literal verb: cut, added, removed, joined, caused, showed, reduced, fixed, compared.

Explainers for beginners are the exception where one analogy usually earns its place. When you use one, say where it stops being true.

> "Your onboarding is a leaky bucket." → "42% of users leave on step 2, where the form asks for billing details before showing the product."

Full list and audit procedure: `references/reframe-and-metaphor.md`.

### 9.7 What not to do in the name of sounding human

- Do not add deliberate errors, typos, or bad grammar.
- Do not add fake personal anecdotes, fake friction, or invented doubt.
- Do not add random sentence-length variation with no reason behind it.
- Do not chop everything into short sentences. That is parataxis, and it is its own tell.
- Do not add fake enthusiasm, fake hesitation, or performed casualness.
- Do not insert slang or profanity that is not the writer's.
- Do not chase detector scores or model-fingerprint folklore (banned opening words, sentence-length thresholds, entropy targets). These are unreliable in both directions, and optimizing against them pushes the prose somewhere worse.

**The overfitting failure.** The list of things to avoid in this skill is long, and the obvious misreading is that good writing is therefore short, clipped, and stripped bare. That is its own machine signature: prose that reads like something carefully avoiding mistakes. Do not turn the output into a checklist of dodged patterns. Keep the exact word when it is the right word, even if it appears on a list. Keep a long sentence when the thought is long.

The working order is: write the thing normally and get the substance right, then remove what sounds machine-made. Not the reverse. Stop when it sounds like a person wrote it, and no later.

## 10. Quality baseline (Google's standards, treated as a floor)

### 10.0 The hard line: scaled content abuse

Everything else in this section is quality guidance. This part is policy, and violating it is a different category of problem from writing a mediocre page.

Google's spam policies define **scaled content abuse** as generating many pages primarily to manipulate Search rankings rather than help users. The policy states it applies no matter how the content is created. That phrase is the whole point: AI-written, human-written, scraped, templated, and outsourced bulk content are all covered equally. Using AI is not the violation. Producing volume without value is.

Two related policies from the same 2024 update:

- **Expired domain abuse:** buying a lapsed domain to exploit its old reputation with low-value content. Using an old domain for a genuinely new, people-first site is fine.
- **Site reputation abuse:** publishing third-party pages on a trusted domain with little first-party oversight, to borrow the host's ranking signals.

What this means in practice for this skill:

- Refuse or push back on bulk page generation across topics the site has no reason to cover. Say plainly that this is the named pattern, not a matter of taste.
- Location-page and template sets ("best [service] in [city]") need genuinely distinct information per page, not a swapped variable. If you cannot write real local detail for each, the set should not exist.
- Every page needs human editorial review before publishing. Google's own "How" guidance suggests disclosing substantial automation where a reader would reasonably wonder how the page was made.
- Never spread the same thin content across several domains to disguise its scale. That is explicitly covered.

Nothing in this skill should be used to produce content at a rate that outpaces the ability to check it.

### 10.1 Self-assessment questions

Google publishes self-assessment questions for helpful, people-first content. Treat them as the minimum bar, not a target to game. The ones that bite hardest:

- Does the content provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis?
- If it draws on other sources, does it avoid simply copying or rewriting them, and instead add substantial value and originality?
- Does it provide insightful information beyond the obvious?
- Would someone want to bookmark it, share it, or recommend it?
- Is it produced with care, or does it look hastily assembled?
- After reading, will the reader feel they learned enough to achieve their goal, or will they need to search again?
- Is it self-evident who wrote it, and does the byline lead somewhere real?

Google also names the warning signs directly: content made mainly to attract search visits, publishing across many unrelated topics hoping something ranks, extensive automation across topics, mainly summarizing others without adding value, and writing to a word count. Google has stated it has no preferred word count.

Trust is the most important element of E-E-A-T. Experience, expertise, and authority all feed into it, and a page does not need all of them, but it must earn trust.

### On ads and AdSense

If the user's goal is ad eligibility, be straight with them:

- **Never promise approval.** No writing method can guarantee it. Anyone claiming otherwise is selling something.
- "Low value content" rejections generally mean the site does not yet offer enough original, useful, trustworthy material, or that it reads as built for monetization rather than for readers.
- Word-count folklore ("every post must be 1000+ words") is not a Google rule and padding to reach it makes the problem worse, not better.
- The fix is site-level, not page-level. Also relevant: a clear site purpose and topic focus, real About and Contact pages, author information, privacy policy, working navigation, and no half-finished sections.
- Say this once, then get on with improving the content. The content work is the same either way, because the underlying quality is the actual thing being judged.

## 11. Content-type rules

Detailed playbooks: `references/content-type-playbooks.md`. Summary:

- **How-to / tutorial:** state prerequisites and expected time up front. Number the steps. Say what each step should produce so the reader can tell if it worked. Name the common failure at the point it occurs, not in a trailing troubleshooting section.
- **Comparison:** decide the criteria that actually matter before listing features. Give a recommendation per reader type. Name what each option is bad at. A comparison where one option wins everything is not a comparison.
- **Review:** disclose the basis. Tested it, used it for months, or read the documentation are three different claims and must not be blurred. Include what it does badly.
- **Explainer:** define before using. Build from what the reader already knows. One analogy, and state where the analogy breaks.
- **Listicle:** every entry needs a distinct reason to exist. Cut to the entries that earn a place, even if the number in the title has to change.
- **News or update:** what changed, who is affected, what to do, when it takes effect. Date it.
- **Landing or product page:** concrete capability over adjectives. Name who it is not for. Every claim checkable.
- **FAQ:** only real questions people ask. Answer in the first sentence. Do not use FAQs as keyword storage.

## 12. Execution workflow

1. **Clarify the job.** Topic, reader, goal, and whether this is new writing, a rewrite, an improvement, or an audit. If the audience or purpose is unclear, ask one focused question rather than guessing.
2. **Ask for first-hand material.** If the user has real experience, data, screenshots, customer questions, or support tickets on this topic, ask for them. This is the single highest-value input and it cannot be invented.
3. **Research** per §3. Search, read the current best answers, go to primary sources, collect concrete specifics.
4. **Plan.** Choose the structure from §4.2. List the sections and the specific fact or example each one carries. If a planned section has no specific content behind it, delete the section.
5. **Draft** per §4, in the voice from §7, applying §8 if the audience is Indian.
6. **Self-edit** against §9. Read the draft as an unsympathetic editor. Cut every sentence that fails the portability test.
7. **Run the final checklist** in §13. Fix what fails, then run it again.
8. **Deliver** the content plus a short note covering: what the page argues or delivers, what sources were used, what you could not verify, and what the user should add from their own experience.

For audits, stop after step 6: name each problem, quote the line, give the fix in a few words, and offer to do the rewrite. Do not score the draft and do not guess whether AI wrote it.

## 13. Final editorial checklist

Run every item before delivering. This is a human editor's pass, not a formality.

**Value**
- [ ] I can state in one sentence what the reader gets here that they would not get from the current top results.
- [ ] Every section carries at least one specific: a number, name, date, mechanism, condition, or worked example.
- [ ] No paragraph can be deleted without losing something.
- [ ] The same point is not made twice in different words.
- [ ] Nothing is padded to reach a length.

**Truth**
- [ ] No invented stats, quotes, studies, prices, or experiences.
- [ ] Every claim that matters is traceable to a real source.
- [ ] No vague attributions, and no position put in a named person's mouth by inference.
- [ ] Every quoted string matches its source word for word.
- [ ] Time-sensitive facts carry a date.
- [ ] Facts, inferences, and opinions are distinguishable.
- [ ] No narration of what I searched for and could not find.
- [ ] Every claimed difference between two things names the mechanism behind it.

**Originality**
- [ ] No phrasing lifted from any source.
- [ ] The structure is mine, chosen for this topic.
- [ ] This is not a reworded summary of one competitor page.

**Human quality**
- [ ] The opening answers, states a problem, or gives real context. No generic runway.
- [ ] Paragraph and sentence length vary because the content varies.
- [ ] Sentence openings are not repetitive.
- [ ] The ending is concrete, not a recap, metaphor, or motivational line.
- [ ] Reading it aloud, the rhythm changes.

**Anti-slop**
- [ ] No banned filler words or delay phrases (§9.1, §9.2).
- [ ] No reframes, including the soft forms and the versions split across two sentences.
- [ ] No faux-insight setups, colon reveals, or fake-depth -ing clauses.
- [ ] No importance puffery or metadiscourse.
- [ ] Analogies within budget and each one passes the permission test.
- [ ] No metaphor verbs attached to abstract objects.
- [ ] No parataxis: three or more blunt statements in a row have been connected or cut.
- [ ] Bold used sparingly. No emoji headings. Lists only where genuinely parallel.
- [ ] Em dashes: none in short copy, at most one or two in long copy.
- [ ] Searched for hallucinated markup (`oaicite`, `contentReference`, `turn0search`) and found none.
- [ ] No deliberate errors added to seem human, and the prose does not read like a checklist of dodged mistakes.

**Indian English (when applicable)**
- [ ] Context and examples suit Indian readers.
- [ ] No inserted grammatical errors and no stereotyped filler.
- [ ] Spelling and number conventions consistent.

**Structure and SEO**
- [ ] Headings describe their sections accurately and could serve as a map.
- [ ] No narrative, mystery, clickbait, or vague-abstraction headings.
- [ ] No heading followed by a restatement of the heading.
- [ ] The title describes the content without exaggerating.
- [ ] Keywords appear because the topic requires the words, not inserted.
- [ ] The page is genuinely worth publishing without search traffic.
- [ ] This page is not one of a templated set where only a variable changes (§10.0).

## 14. Failure cases

**The user asks for 20 articles on unrelated topics.**
Say what the problem is: this is the pattern Google's systems and ad reviewers both treat as a warning sign, and it rarely works. Offer to build a focused set around the site's actual subject instead. If they still want breadth, write fewer, better pages and say why.

**The user wants first-hand experience the writer does not have.**
Do not fabricate it. Explain that invented testing is the fastest way to lose reader trust and is a real risk if a reader checks. Offer three alternatives: interview the user about their real experience, build value through synthesis and accuracy instead, or clearly frame the page as a researched overview.

**A required fact cannot be verified.**
Do not fill the gap with a plausible number. Flag it, write around it, or leave a marked placeholder for the user to supply.

**The topic genuinely has a short answer.**
Write the short answer. Tell the user why the page is short. Suggest either leaving it short or expanding the scope to a related question that actually needs depth. Do not pad.

**The user asks for content on a YMYL topic without expertise.**
Raise it. Health, finance, legal, and safety content is held to a stricter standard. Write from primary sources, date everything, state jurisdiction, avoid individual advice, and recommend the page carry review by someone qualified.

**The user asks to make content "undetectable" by AI detectors.**
Explain plainly that detectors are unreliable, that gaming them degrades the writing, and that this skill improves the writing instead. Then do that. The result usually reads as human because it is written like a human wrote it.

**The user wants a competitor's page "rewritten."**
Do not reword it. Use it as one input, research the topic independently, and build an original page that covers what theirs misses.

**Existing content is already good.**
Say so. Suggest targeted improvements. Do not rewrite working prose to demonstrate effort.
